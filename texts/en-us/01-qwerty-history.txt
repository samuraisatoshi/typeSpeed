The Origins of the QWERTY Keyboard

The QWERTY keyboard layout takes its name from the first six
letter keys on the top row, read from left to right. It was
designed in the early 1870s for mechanical typewriters built by
the Sholes and Glidden company, later manufactured by Remington.

One popular explanation claims the layout was arranged to slow
typists down and reduce the frequent jamming of adjacent
mechanical type bars. Historians debate this theory, since some
research suggests the design instead made common letter pairs
easier to strike using alternating hands.

Despite the arrival of electronic keyboards and touchscreens,
QWERTY has remained the dominant layout for English and many
other languages for well over a century. Alternative layouts
such as Dvorak and Colemak were later proposed to improve typing
speed and reduce finger movement, but QWERTY's installed base
and muscle memory advantage have kept it firmly in place.

Learning to type without looking at the keys, commonly called
touch typing, allows a typist to reach higher speeds and lower
error rates than hunting and pecking. Consistent practice on
real text and code is one of the most effective ways to build
that muscle memory.
